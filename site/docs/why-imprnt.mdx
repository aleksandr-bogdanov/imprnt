In 2026, I wanted to **buy a bike** to ride to work. A normal person would have walked into a local bike store, pointed at one, and took it home.
Instead of that I started googling about bikes and ended up knowing about gears, suspension travel and racing bikes types more than anyone
looking for a bike to commute will ever need. In the end I bought a bike but it took me two weeks of thorough investigation.

<span id="beat-claude" class="beat-anchor"></span>

If you see yourself in that pattern at least slightly - keep on reading because there's more.
That's how I do everything. A hobby, a purchase, a decision - I have to understand it inside out before I act. At first it was Google, then it was Claude. And Claude felt like a friend who is very smart and knowledgeable about everything.

But this friend had no memory. Every chat started from zero. I would re-explain my setup, my constraints, the thing I decided last week, and by the next session all of it was gone. Every time, I was briefing a brilliant stranger who happened to have read the entire internet. It was intelligent all right, it just had **no memory** that lasted past the end of the chat.

<span id="beat-pai" class="beat-anchor"></span>

## PAI was perfect, for someone else

Then I found PAI, I do not even remember where. It did the one thing I wanted most. It remembered. It carried context across sessions, gave me a structure for my goals and values it called TELOS, and had the depth I had been missing. For a while it was exactly right.

<span id="beat-v5" class="beat-anchor"></span>

Version 5 is where we parted ways. It grew machinery I never asked for. A Pulse dashboard that ran on its own schedule. A telegram bot wired in permanently when I had no use for one. A whole framework for ideal goals and ideal criteria, a nice idea that was more machine than my life needed. None of it was wrong. It was **built around its author's work**, and he writes about AI for a living. A tool that helps him write all day is the right one for him to build. Mine is a different job.

<span id="beat-tokens" class="beat-anchor"></span>

Then I took it to work, where there is no flat subscription, only a metered token budget where every token is real money. On the first load, against an almost empty account, the machinery burned tokens before I had typed anything useful.

<WhyTokenWall />

The problem had changed shape. The model used to forget too fast. Now the parts I never asked for were eating the budget I needed to work, and I could not pull them out.

<span id="beat-rule" class="beat-anchor"></span>

## The rule that fixed it

Around then a colleague reframed it for me. He was ruthless about tokens, and his rule was simple. The model is a **peer** you bring in for judgment, and the software you write is the servant that does the work.

<WhyQuote>
You would not pay a sharp colleague to re-read your whole inbox every morning to find the three emails that matter. You would have them write the filter once. After that the filter runs every morning, for free, **forever**.
</WhyQuote>

<WhyPeerServant />

<span id="beat-imprnt" class="beat-anchor"></span>

That was my whole PAI problem in one line. I had been treating a brilliant peer like a servant, paying full price to redo the same mechanical work over and over, so it got slow and expensive. imprnt is that idea flipped around. The model reads a source and files a clean note **once**, with judgment. Plain local code does the searching and the checking a thousand times after, for nothing.

## What imprnt will never do

There is one rule I will not break, because breaking it is exactly what pushed me out of PAI. imprnt will never be shaped around only my problems, and it will never hand you a tool you did not ask for. The default setup ships with almost nothing. Your plain Claude stays plain. Everything is **opt-in**, and anything you add you can remove with a single command. If a part of imprnt does not fit your work, you were never meant to carry it. You take what serves you and leave the rest.

If that is the assistant you have been wanting, [start here](/getting-started/), or browse the [plugins](/plugins/) and add only the ones you need.
